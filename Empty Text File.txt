## Game Design & Technical Architecture — Living Project Specification

**Document status:** Initial design baseline  
**Genre:** 2D top-down survival, exploration, and modular base-building  
**Primary visual reference:** MewnBase  
**Technology target:** JavaScript ES Modules, Phaser 3, Vite  
**Platform target:** Desktop browser first  
**Team:** Two active contributors, supported by AI coding agents

# 1. High concept

The player is a lone astronaut stranded on the Moon after an emergency landing. Their spacecraft is damaged but can serve as the first habitat. Earth initially estimates that a rescue mission will arrive in 30 days. The astronaut must restore essential systems, explore nearby terrain, recover equipment, establish local resource production, and keep the base operational.

The game is about **gradually turning a fragile emergency shelter into a functioning lunar outpost**. It is not a large colony simulator and not a factory automation game.

### Design pillars

1. **Understandable survival:** every critical resource has a clear purpose and visible consequence.
2. **Hands-on early game:** the astronaut personally explores, collects, repairs, and transports.
3. **Progressive automation:** a recovered rover and later equipment reduce repetitive manual work.
4. **Modular base:** rooms and equipment form a readable, expandable habitat.
5. **Science-inspired fiction:** real lunar science informs mechanics, while invented events are clearly fictional.
6. **Small, finishable scope:** prioritize one polished playable scenario over many unfinished systems.

# 2. Player experience and camera

- 2D top-down perspective.
- The astronaut moves freely around a single continuous outdoor map.
- Camera follows the player and remains inside world bounds.
- The player can enter or interact with habitat modules; interior implementation may initially be abstracted as room screens or interaction panels.
- The art direction is friendly, readable, slightly cartoon-like, with clear silhouettes and restrained sci-fi details.
- Visual reference: MewnBase. Do not copy its assets, UI, or exact layouts.

### Controls (initial target)

| Input | Action |
|---|---|
| WASD / arrows | Move |
| E | Interact with nearby object |
| Left mouse | Select/build/place UI items |
| Esc | Pause / close current panel |
| Tab or I | Inventory |

Controls must be configurable later; do not build a full keybinding menu in the first prototype.

# 3. Core gameplay loop

1. Receive a task, clue, or status update from Earth or the base computer.
2. Explore the nearby lunar surface.
3. Find a resource deposit, wreck, research site, or useful object.
4. Collect, scan, repair, or solve a small environmental problem.
5. Return resources to the habitat.
6. Process resources using installed equipment.
7. Build or repair modules and improve life-support reliability.
8. Prepare for the next event or environmental challenge.

The player should usually have one clear short-term objective and one longer-term survival pressure.

# 4. Narrative premise and progression

## Starting situation

A crewed mission was expected to perform a short lunar research operation. During landing, the lander suffers a major malfunction. The astronaut survives; the propulsion/return systems and communications equipment are damaged. The spacecraft remains usable as a basic shelter, but its life-support and power capabilities are limited.

After restoring a minimal communications link, Earth reports that a rescue mission is being prepared and estimates **30 days** until arrival.

The campaign's exact win day is a design choice. Recommended version:
- Initial estimate: rescue in 30 days.
- Around Day 20, Earth sends an update: the rescue window has improved and arrival is expected on Day 25.
- Win condition: survive until the rescue vehicle arrives on Day 25 and complete a final landing/beacon objective.

This creates a meaningful early expectation and a late-game acceleration without contradicting the timeline. If the team prefers a 20-day campaign, Earth must instead announce a 10-day acceleration.

## Suggested story beats (adjustable)

| Approx. day | Beat | Gameplay purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Emergency landing; inspect damaged lander | Tutorial, basic movement/interactions |
| 2 | Restore a limited communications link | Introduce Earth messages and objectives |
| 3–5 | Locate nearby ice clues and recover basic supplies | Exploration and water chain |
| 4–7 | Discover a disabled research rover | Introduce repair and gradual automation |
| 7–9 | Repair rover enough to scan/transport | Reduce manual hauling |
| 9–12 | Find a remote biological research outpost | First expedition / narrative mystery |
| 10–14 | Recover a viable fungal culture and starter substrate | Unlock food production |
| 15–19 | Expand power storage and prepare for low-sun period | Mid-game resource planning |
| 20 | Earth announces earlier rescue window | Raise stakes and set final objective |
| 21–24 | Stabilize base, repair beacon, prepare landing area | Endgame checklist |
| 25 | Rescue arrives | Victory |

These are narrative targets, not hard-coded timers. The playable prototype can compress days substantially.

# 5. Science-inspired systems

Scientific accuracy should support the fiction, not make the game tedious. The game may simplify processes, but must not present speculative or fictional facilities as documented real lunar installations.

## 5.1 Regolith and materials

Lunar regolith is fragmented rock and dust, not ordinary soil. It contains oxygen chemically bound in minerals and elements including silicon, iron, and aluminium.

Potential gameplay progression:
- Early: collect small quantities of regolith manually.
- Mid: rover-mounted scoop or drill collects larger quantities.
- Later: processing equipment separates useful material.
- Advanced: high-temperature processing / molten regolith electrolysis is a costly research unlock.

Do not make raw regolith instantly become metal or breathable oxygen. Processing requires power, heat, equipment, and time.

## 5.2 Water ice

Water ice is believed to exist in some permanently shadowed lunar regions, especially near the poles. It is not guaranteed to be easily accessible at every landing site.

Gameplay chain:
1. Receive a broad location clue from Earth.
2. Use a scanner or repaired rover to identify a promising deposit.
3. Extract ice-bearing regolith.
4. Heat it inside a sealed extraction unit.
5. Capture and condense water vapour.
6. Filter/purify the collected water before life-support use.

The game may simplify filtration to a filter cartridge and a processing timer. Filters wear out and need replacement or regeneration.

## 5.3 Water electrolysis, oxygen, and hydrogen

Purified water can be split by electrolysis into oxygen and hydrogen. This process consumes electrical energy.

Gameplay chain:
- Purified water + electricity -> oxygen + hydrogen.
- Oxygen supports breathing and habitat pressure systems.
- Hydrogen can be stored as a chemical feedstock or used in a suitable burner/process heater, subject to an oxygen supply and proper equipment.
- Do not imply hydrogen is a free energy source: producing it required energy.

A regenerative fuel cell is a separate device: it can combine stored hydrogen and oxygen to generate electricity and water. It is useful for energy storage, but conversion losses mean it is not perpetual energy.

## 5.4 Power and lunar day/night

Solar panels are a plausible early power source, but output depends on sunlight, orientation, dust, and local terrain. Long lunar nights create a major storage challenge.

Gameplay progression:
- Start with damaged or limited imported solar panels and a small battery.
- Repair panels and reduce unnecessary loads.
- Build larger storage.
- Later research locally manufactured solar-cell components from regolith-derived materials.

A lunar night is roughly 14 Earth days in many locations, but exact illumination depends on location and terrain. For a short game, use an intentionally compressed day/night schedule and label it as a gameplay time scale.

## 5.5 Food: fungal culture and hydroponics

There is no reason to assume useful wild mushrooms or fungal spores are naturally available on the Moon. A viable culture must be brought from Earth or recovered from a fictional prior experiment.

NASA has studied fungi in space-related research, but the game’s abandoned lunar biological facility is **fictional**. Present it as a fictional robotic research outpost inspired by real space-biology research, not as a real NASA lunar installation.

Suggested gameplay:
- Initial emergency food supplies cover the first days.
- Earth hints at a nearby automated biological experiment.
- The player reaches the site, restores power, bypasses a damaged access system, and retrieves a sealed viable fungal culture plus a starter growth substrate.
- The habitat's compact fungal grow chamber produces a modest food supply over time.
- Later, a hydroponic module can grow leafy crops from seeds and nutrient solution. Seeds and nutrient supplies were part of the mission payload or recovered cargo.

Fungi are not a complete, magically self-sufficient diet. Keep emergency rations relevant and treat the grow chamber as a supplement.

# 6. Rover progression

A disabled robotic rover is found near the landing area or along an Earth-marked route. It was part of an earlier fictional automated survey mission.

Repair progression:
1. Restore basic power and movement.
2. Restore short-range communication with the habitat.
3. Unlock terrain scanning / resource detection.
4. Restore scoop or drill for regolith and ice-bearing material.
5. Restore cargo compartment for hauling.
6. Optional late upgrade: automated trips between known points.

The rover should not instantly automate everything. Each repair stage requires a small quest, components, or resources. Its primary purpose is to shift the player from repetitive hauling toward exploration and base management.

# 7. Exploration, quests, and environmental storytelling

Use clues rather than exposition dumps:
- Earth messages with coordinates and partial scientific explanations.
- Damaged rover logs.
- Research terminal entries.
- Audio/text logs from the fictional biological outpost.
- Equipment labels and warning signs.
- Unusual terrain formations and abandoned cargo.
- Optional discoveries that reveal why the previous robotic mission stopped.

Each expedition should contain at least one meaningful action: restore power, reroute a circuit, find a key component, scan a location, manage a hazard, or solve a simple spatial puzzle. Avoid “walk there, pick up item, walk back” as the only quest pattern.

Scientific references should be phrased carefully. If a detail is not verified, mark it as fictional inspiration in the design rather than claiming NASA did it.

# 8. Resources and production chains

Keep the first playable version small. Candidate resource set:

| Resource | Main use | Acquisition |
|---|---|---|
| Regolith | Construction / processing feedstock | Manual collection, rover |
| Metal parts | Repairs and equipment | Starting stock, salvage, later processing |
| Ice | Water source | Scanner + extraction |
| Purified water | Drinking, electrolysis, agriculture | Ice processing + filtration |
| Oxygen | Breathing / life support | Electrolysis |
| Hydrogen | Storage / process fuel / fuel cell | Electrolysis |
| Power | All powered equipment | Solar + batteries / fuel cell |
| Food | Survival | Rations + fungal grow chamber |

Avoid adding dozens of materials until the core loop is fun.

### Key dependencies

- Ice -> raw water -> filtration -> purified water.
- Purified water + power -> electrolysis -> oxygen + hydrogen.
- Hydrogen + oxygen -> fuel cell -> electricity + water (with losses).
- Regolith + heat + advanced processor -> useful materials / oxygen (late game).
- Fungal culture + substrate + water + controlled environment -> food over time.
- Solar panels -> power; batteries store power but do not generate it.

# 9. Survival pressures

The first version should model only a few understandable pressures:
- Oxygen reserve / consumption.
- Food reserve / consumption.
- Power reserve / production.
- Water reserve / consumption.

Health can be a simple fail-state buffer in the first prototype. Temperature, radiation, dust contamination, pressure leaks, and equipment wear are later features (P2), not launch requirements.

The UI must communicate why a resource is declining and what action can fix it. Avoid invisible death timers.

# 10. Base building and modularity

The base begins as the damaged lander/habitat. The player adds connected room modules.

Initial module types:
- Habitat / command module.
- Storage.
- Workshop.
- Water processing module.
- Power module.
- Fungal grow chamber.
- Later: laboratory / hydroponics / advanced regolith processor.

A module has:
- Unique ID.
- Type.
- Grid position / footprint.
- Rotation (optional later).
- Health / condition.
- Power demand or production.
- Operational state.
- Optional input/output resource definitions.

For the first version, modules can be placed on a grid and connected through door/connector points. Do not implement complex room pressure simulation, pipe routing, or electrical cable routing yet. Use a simple “connected to base” rule.

# 11. User interface

Main menu:
- New Game

- Settings
- Credits

In-game HUD:
- Day / mission timer.
- Oxygen, food, water, power.
- Current objective.
- Interaction prompt.
- Inventory shortcut.
- Build menu shortcut.

Panels:
- Inventory.
- Nearby object interaction.
- Building placement.
- Machine status / recipe.
- Earth communications / quest log.

Keep the interface readable at common desktop resolutions. UI should be separate from world rendering and should not be hard-coded into gameplay systems.

# 12. Technical architecture

## Stack

- JavaScript ES Modules.
- Phaser 3 for rendering, scenes, input, camera, physics/collisions where useful.
- Vite for local development and production bundling.
- HTML/CSS for page shell and any DOM-based overlays.
- Git for version control.
- GitHub private repository for collaboration.

Do not introduce React, a backend, multiplayer, or a database unless a later requirement justifies it.

## Responsibilities and boundaries

- `main.js`: create and start the Phaser game. No gameplay rules.
- `game/`: global configuration, constants, event names.
- `scenes/`: lifecycle and composition of screens. Scenes coordinate systems; they should not contain every rule.
- `entities/`: state and behavior of individual world objects.
- `systems/`: reusable gameplay rules (movement, inventory, resource processing, quests).
- `world/`: map, world boundaries, camera.
- `data/`: declarative definitions for items, buildings, recipes, quests. Prefer data-driven content over giant switch statements.
- `ui/`: display and user input for panels. UI calls system APIs; it does not directly mutate unrelated game state.
- `utils/`: small pure helper functions.

## Core data concepts

Use simple, serializable objects where possible.

**GameState**
- day
- astronaut position
- inventory
- resource balances
- placed buildings
- machine states
- rover repair stage
- quest flags
- survival meters

**ItemDefinition**
- id
- displayName
- category
- stackLimit
- description

**BuildingDefinition**
- id
- displayName
- footprint
- buildCost
- powerUse / powerOutput
- recipe or function
- unlock condition

**RecipeDefinition**
- id
- input resources
- output resources
- processing duration
- power requirement

**QuestDefinition**
- id
- title
- objective steps
- prerequisites
- completion rewards
- associated message / log

Avoid overengineering with complex inheritance, dependency injection frameworks, or an ECS unless the project genuinely needs one.

## System interaction rules

- Systems own gameplay rules and state changes.
- UI requests actions through systems.
- Entities represent in-world objects and expose clear methods.
- Data files define content; systems interpret the data.
- Use Phaser events or a small internal event bus for loose coupling where useful.
- Keep save data serializable; do not attempt to serialize Phaser display objects.
- Do not store duplicate copies of the same resource balance in UI and gameplay state.

### P1 — First gameplay loop
1. Inventory and manual resource collection.
2. Basic interaction system.

5. Day counter and simple survival consumption.

### P2 — Campaign systems

2. Electrolysis and hydrogen storage.

5. Additional modules, logs, events, and ending.

# 13. Scope control: what NOT to build first

Do not implement in the initial prototype:
- Multiplayer.
- Procedural infinite world.
- Complex physics simulation.
- Full lunar orbital mechanics.
- Detailed pressure / oxygen simulation per room.
- Complex power grids or pipes.
- Full hydroponic crop simulation.
- Advanced regolith metallurgy.
- Dozens of resources.
- Mobile support.
- Online accounts or server backend.

# 14. Definition of a successful first prototype

A player can:
1. Start from a main menu.
2. Enter a lunar surface scene.
3. Move the astronaut around.
4. See the camera follow.
5. Approach an object and interact.
6. See a basic HUD and objective.
7. Build and run the project locally with documented commands.

The first prototype is not yet the full game. It proves the technical foundation and visual direction.

# 15. Team workflow

- Keep `main` stable.
- Use short feature branches for substantial changes.
- Make small commits with meaningful messages.
- Pull before starting work.
- Avoid having two people edit the same file simultaneously.
- Test `npm run build` before merging.
- Do not commit secrets, tokens, local environment files, or generated dependency folders.
- Every AI agent must update `CHANGELOG.md` after it changes project files.

# 16. Open design decisions

These are intentionally unresolved and should be decided after the first prototype:
- Exact map dimensions and landing-site location.
- Pixel art versus smooth hand-drawn vector-like sprites.
- Whether interiors are separate scenes or camera transitions.
- Exact survival consumption rates and day length.
- Whether the win day is Day 25 or another agreed value.
- Whether the first version uses placeholder art or a small custom asset pack.
- Exact number of buildable modules in the first playable campaign.
